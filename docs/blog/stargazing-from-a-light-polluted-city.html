Stargazing From a Light-Polluted City: A Beginner's Notes

September 2, 2026 · 4 min read · Sarthak Kulshrestha · Astronomy

  • Astronomy
  • Beginners

A seed post for the astronomy section — I'll expand it as I add more observing notes.

The most common reason people give up on astronomy before they start is that they think they need a dark-sky site and a large telescope. You need neither to begin. A bright city or suburban sky does close off part of the hobby, but a surprising amount stays open — and the parts that stay open are a good place to learn.

Know what your sky can actually show you

The Bortle scale runs from class 1 (a truly dark site, the Milky Way casting shadows) to class 9 (an inner-city sky). Most cities sit around 7 to 9. Practically, that means the Milky Way is gone, and faint galaxies and nebulae are gone. But the Moon, the bright planets, double stars, the brighter star clusters, and the main constellation patterns are all still there. Set your expectations to those targets and light pollution stops feeling like a disappointment and starts being just a constraint.

Start with the Moon

The Moon is the best possible first target: it is bright enough that light pollution is irrelevant, and it is full of detail. Look along the terminator, the line dividing the lit and unlit halves, where the shadows are long and craters and mountains stand out in relief. Counterintuitively, a full Moon is the worst time to look — it is flat and glary. First quarter, a few days after new Moon, is ideal.

The bright planets

Jupiter's four largest moons are visible in steadily-held binoculars, and its cloud bands show in a small telescope. Saturn's rings resolve in almost any telescope above about 50x magnification, and that first view tends to be the moment people are hooked. Venus runs through phases like a tiny moon. Mars is small and only rewards patience near opposition. None of these targets care how bright your sky is.

Protect your dark adaptation

Your eyes take 20 to 30 minutes to adapt to darkness, and a single glance at a phone screen resets the process. Use a dim red light for reading charts, and turn your phone brightness all the way down or use a red-screen mode. Even in the city, this is the difference between seeing a handful of stars and seeing a few hundred.

Tools, in the order worth buying them

  1. A planisphere or a free sky-map app, to learn where things are and when they are up.
  2. Binoculars — 7x50 or 10x50. This is the highest value-for-money tool in the whole hobby: good for the Moon, wide star fields, the Pleiades, and bright comets and conjunctions.
  3. Only then a telescope, and a simple one. A small Dobsonian gives you more usable aperture than a wobbly tripod refractor at the same price.

Photographing from a bright sky

Holding a phone camera up to the eyepiece works startlingly well for the Moon — line up the lens, tap to focus, and take the shot. For wide-field work, a camera on a fixed tripod at one to two seconds catches constellations and bright planetary conjunctions. Stacking many short exposures in free software pulls faint signal out of a washed-out sky.

Closing thought

Treat the city sky as a training sky. You learn the constellations, you learn how your equipment behaves, and you build the habit of observing carefully and patiently. All of that transfers directly the first night you get somewhere genuinely dark — and you will already know what you are looking at.
